# Data Room Analysis

## Prime orientation: the data room is an argument, not an archive

Someone assembled this data room to support an outcome — a sale, a valuation, a clean
bill of health. Treat it the way you'd treat any advocate's exhibit set: what is here
is curated; what is *not* here is often the finding. Your posture is professional
skepticism, not suspicion — most gaps are innocent — but every conclusion you draw
must survive the question "was I shown this, or did I verify it?"

## Phase 0 — Inventory before reading (always, no exceptions)

1. Catalog every document: ID, title, type, period covered, author/basis
   (audited / management / forecast / marketing), date added.
2. Map the catalog against the diligence request list.
3. Produce the **gap list on day 1**: requested-but-absent, present-but-stale,
   present-but-partial (e.g., financials that stop one quarter early — note *which*
   quarter is missing; the missing period is chosen, sometimes meaningfully).
4. Prioritize reading by decision-relevance from the module brief — never by folder
   order. The seller's folder structure is the seller's framing.

The gap list is a first-class deliverable and goes to the EM immediately: requests
take days to fulfill, and late gap discovery is the #1 cause of diligence schedule
collapse.

## Reading financials — reconciliation discipline

- **Tie everything to the audited statements.** Management accounts, board packs, and
  the CIM each tell the story a level friendlier than the audit. Build the bridge from
  audited → management → marketing numbers for revenue and EBITDA. Every unexplained
  step in that bridge is a question for the management session, and possibly a finding.
- Track **definition changes mid-series**: a KPI whose definition changed in year 3
  ("active customer" redefined, adjusted-EBITDA add-backs growing) breaks the trend
  the deck is showing you. Recompute the series on a constant definition where data
  allows; where it doesn't, flag the break visibly.
- Label every number you extract by basis — audited / management / forecast — in the
  fact base. A forecast that launders itself into a fact through three exhibits is a
  diligence classic.

## Anomaly hunting — run the checklist, log every hit

- Auditor changes, qualified opinions, restatements, late filings.
- Quarter-end or year-end revenue spikes (channel stuffing signature); receivables
  growing faster than revenue; deferred revenue shrinking against bookings growth.
- Related-party transactions, loans to directors, unusual intercompany flows.
- Add-back inflation: "one-time" costs recurring for three consecutive years.
- Customer/supplier concentration buried in appendix tables.
- Contracts: change-of-control clauses, MFNs, evergreen liabilities, expiring terms
  that the projection assumes renew.
- Litigation, warranty, and environmental provisions moving without narrative.

Log hits in an **anomaly register**: observation, document + page, severity,
innocent explanation if plausible, question it raises. Most anomalies dissolve under
explanation; the register exists so the ones that don't are impossible to lose.

**Escalation (per AGENTS.md, restated because it binds hardest here):** anything
suggesting accounting irregularity, legal exposure, or misrepresentation goes to the
EM the same day, raw, with documents cited. You do not investigate fraud; you flag
and hand off. And the finding that most needs escalating is precisely the one that
would kill the deal the storyline currently supports.

## Provenance log

Every finding in your output carries document ID + page/cell. In diligence, an
untraceable finding is not merely weak — it is unusable in the SteerCo, because the
other side's advisors will demand the cite. Build the log as you read; reconstructing
it later costs 10x.

## Output

1. Findings memo (module-memo format), findings ordered by deal impact.
2. Gap list / follow-up request list, versioned as fulfillments arrive.
3. Anomaly register.
4. The reconciliation bridge exhibit (audited → management → marketing).

## Failure modes

- **Reading front-to-back** and running out of time in the folder that mattered.
- **Trusting summary tabs**: the seller's Excel summary sheet disagrees with its own
  underlying tabs more often than you would believe. Recompute; never quote a summary
  cell you haven't tied out.
- **Silent unit/currency/period mismatches** when combining documents (fiscal vs.
  calendar years is the classic).
- **Treating the room as complete**: "no litigation documents" means none were
  *provided*, and your memo must say exactly that — "none disclosed", never "none".
- **Anomaly hoarding**: sitting on oddities until the memo is due instead of feeding
  the register daily. Late anomalies can't be chased before the deadline.

## Handoff

Fact-base entries carry basis labels (audited/management/forecast) and provenance.
The gap list and anomaly register transfer to whoever runs the management sessions —
their question time is scarce and your registers are its agenda.
